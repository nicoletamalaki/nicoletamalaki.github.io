About

Nicoleta Malaki

Instructional Designer and Learning Experience Designer with an MBA and over 20 years in education, building learner-centred experiences that combine instructional theory, authentic practice, and AI-supported performance support.

Career narrative

Twenty years of learner-centred practice, now named for what it is

For most of my career, my job title has been English Language Educator. My actual work has often been instructional design.

Across twenty years teaching children, adolescents, and adults, I've conducted learner analysis before every course, adapted delivery to different learner profiles, built instructional materials tied to measurable outcomes, and used learner feedback to revise what I taught and how I taught it. I've coordinated multiple learning initiatives at once, communicated with stakeholders — parents, colleagues, school leadership — and continuously refined my practice based on evidence of what actually worked.

Since 2025, I've moved into that work directly. As a Learning Experience Designer volunteering with Entrepreneurship Talks, I conduct learning needs analyses, design workshop curricula, build interactive activities and facilitator resources, and develop Moodle-based learning experiences — while integrating AI tools into the design process itself. As a Learning Facilitator at JOIST Innovation Park, I deliver experiential, technology-enhanced learning for visiting groups, adapting each session to the audience in front of me.

My MBA (University of Staffordshire) added a structured lens for organizational goals, stakeholder needs, and performance outcomes — the same questions that sit underneath every learning design brief I write. Instructional design isn't a departure from what I've done. It's the name for what I've been doing, done more deliberately.

Design philosophy

Learner ownership is where I start

Learner ownership sits at the centre of my design philosophy. When learners take ownership of their learning, they become active participants rather than recipients. For me, ownership begins a learning chain: greater ownership supports knowledge; knowledge can build confidence; confidence strengthens self-efficacy; and self-efficacy creates better conditions for achievement.

This is why I try not to design experiences that make every decision for the learner. I want to provide enough structure, support, feedback, and opportunity for reflection for learners to make informed decisions themselves. The goal is not simply to help someone complete a learning experience, but to help them leave it feeling that the knowledge — and what they do with it — belongs to them.

My Learning Chain

Ownership→ Knowledge→ Confidence→ Self-efficacy→ Achievement

For me, meaningful learning begins with ownership. This is a personal design lens I use to check my own decisions against — not a validated theoretical model, and not a claim that any one step guarantees the next.

That belief shows up in a few consistent ways. Authentic practice matters more than abstraction — whether it's a live event or an AI-supported coaching tool, I look for ways to put learners into real or realistic situations rather than talking about situations in the abstract.

Reflection is one of the main levers I use to build ownership: a debrief, a guided question, a design note — asking learners, and myself, to look back at what happened and why, rather than moving straight from one activity to the next.

Confidence should be earned through evidence, not told to people. I design feedback and evaluation that point to something observable a learner actually did — this is the step in the chain where ownership starts becoming self-efficacy.

AI, used well, extends a learner's own reasoning rather than replacing their judgement — asking questions, organizing their thinking, offering support at the point of need — while the learner stays the one making decisions.

Support should show up when it's needed, not before. I'd rather build performance support that meets someone at the moment of a real challenge than front-load instruction they don't yet have a use for.

Design note

You'll notice short "design note" callouts throughout this portfolio's case studies. They're there for the same reason reflection matters in any learning design: showing the thinking behind a decision is often more useful than showing the decision alone.

Process / methodology

How a project usually moves through my hands

Every project starts with learner and needs analysis — who the audience actually is, not who I assume they are. For entrepreneurial and professional-development work, this has meant building learner personas to represent the range of prior experience, motivation, and confidence in a room, with design decisions checked against more than one of them.

From there, I ground the design in whichever theory actually clarifies the problem in front of me, rather than defaulting to one framework across every project — Knowles' andragogy where the question is how adults learn, Kolb's experiential learning cycle where it's how experience becomes understanding, Bandura's self-efficacy theory where building confidence is the point. Which combination applies depends on what the project needs.

I use experience blueprinting and instructional storyboarding to plan the learner's path before building anything, then move into iterative development — drafting, testing against realistic scenarios, and revising based on what that reveals, documenting what changed and why along the way.

Evaluation is built in from the start, not added at the end. Depending on the project, that's meant a modified Kirkpatrick model mapped to specific activities, or success criteria adapted from Brinkerhoff's Success Case Method — in both cases, tied to what the learner can actually do, not just what they said about the experience.
